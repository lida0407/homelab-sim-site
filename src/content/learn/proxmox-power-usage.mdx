## The short answer

Proxmox doesn't measure watts, and a VM can't see real power at all. Measure the **host** from outside, then work through the usual idle-power settings, measuring after each change. Most of a homelab host's energy goes into idling, so idle watts are the number to bring down.

## Step 1: measure

| Method | What it measures | How |
|---|---|---|
| Plug-in meter / smart plug | Whole host at the wall (what you pay for) | Read it at idle and under typical load; a smart plug can log it over days |
| UPS through NUT | Everything on that UPS | `upsc <ups>@<host>`: use `ups.realpower` if present, otherwise `ups.load` (%) × `ups.realpower.nominal` ÷ 100 |
| IPMI / BMC (rack servers) | Whole server, from its own sensors | `ipmitool dcmi power reading` |
| RAPL (Intel and recent AMD) | CPU package (and DRAM) only | `turbostat --Summary --show PkgWatt --interval 5` on the host, not in a VM |

RAPL is good for comparing settings but leaves out disks, fans, NICs and power-supply losses. For cost, use the wall, the UPS or IPMI.

## Step 2: check the CPU's power settings

On the Proxmox host shell:

```bash
cat /sys/devices/system/cpu/cpu0/cpufreq/scaling_driver     # intel_pstate, amd-pstate-epp, acpi-cpufreq …
cat /sys/devices/system/cpu/cpu0/cpufreq/scaling_governor   # performance, powersave, ondemand, schedutil …
cat /sys/devices/system/cpu/amd_pstate/status 2>/dev/null   # AMD only: active, guided or passive
cat /proc/cmdline                                           # any governor / pstate overrides
```

- With **intel_pstate** or **amd-pstate in active mode**, only `performance` and `powersave` are offered. `powersave` still scales up under load; the energy preference (EPP) decides how eagerly.
- With **amd-pstate in guided mode**, the classic governors (`schedutil`, `ondemand` …) return.
- Try `powersave` (or `schedutil`) for a day: `echo powersave | tee /sys/devices/system/cpu/cpu*/cpufreq/scaling_governor` (lost at reboot). Measure idle watts before and after. To keep it, add `cpufreq.default_governor=powersave` to the kernel command line and run `proxmox-boot-tool refresh` (systemd-boot) or `update-grub` (GRUB).

Don't trust a default; check what your host actually runs.

## Step 3: cut idle watts (measure after each)

1. **BIOS: allow deep C-states and PCIe ASPM.** Many servers and some desktop boards ship with them off or in a "performance" profile. This is often the biggest single change.
2. **`powertop --auto-tune`** (`apt install powertop`): turns on power management for USB, SATA and PCIe devices. Test for a day before making it permanent; some NICs and USB devices dislike it.
3. **Spin down idle disks.** `hdparm -S 120 /dev/sdX` sets a 10-minute spindown (values 1–240 are multiples of 5 seconds). Don't do this on disks a ZFS pool or a VM touches every few minutes: constant spin-ups cost more than they save.
4. **Remove what idles.** Unused HBAs, 10G NICs, GPUs and extra drives each draw several watts or more around the clock.
5. **Stop or consolidate idle VMs.** Busy-polling guests and many small VMs keep cores out of deep idle. Containers (LXC) usually cost less than a full VM for the same service.
6. **Fans and BMC profiles.** On rack servers, a "minimum power" or "optimal" profile in the management controller can lower fan speed and idle draw.

## Step 4: is new hardware worth it?

Two small tools:

**Savings from a change.** Enter idle watts before and after, plus your price per kWh, to get watts saved and money saved per month and per year. Example: 150 W → 30 W at €0.30/kWh saves 120 W, **≈ €26 a month, ≈ €315 a year**.

**Payback of a replacement.** Enter the old and new average watts, the new hardware's price and what you'd get for the old box. The output is months to pay back (or "never" if the new box doesn't draw less). Example: 150 W → 30 W, €400 new, €100 resale → **≈ 11 months**.
