## The short answer

**Tokens per joule = output tokens per second ÷ watts at the wall.** A watt is one joule per second, so the seconds cancel. Multiply by 3,600,000 (the joules in a kWh) and you have **tokens per kWh**. From there, the electricity cost of a million tokens is:

> cost per 1M tokens = 1,000,000 ÷ tokens per kWh × your price per kWh

Tokens per second tells you how fast a box is. Tokens per kWh tells you what it costs to run.

The last three rows are the point of the optional inputs. A box that generates for two hours and idles for twenty-two spends most of its energy doing nothing, so the cost per million tokens you actually produce is several times the "while generating" figure.

## How to measure the two inputs

**Tokens per second (output, generation only).**
- Ollama: `ollama run <model> --verbose` prints the eval rate after each answer. Through the API, the response carries `eval_count` (tokens generated) and `eval_duration` (nanoseconds): tok/s = eval_count ÷ eval_duration × 10⁹.
- Use a realistic prompt and a reasonably filled context. Prompt processing (prefill) is a different, much faster number; don't mix the two.
- Average several runs over a few minutes, not one short answer.

**Watts at the wall.**
- A smart plug or a plug-in meter on the AI box, read while it generates. This is the number your bill sees.
- Your UPS, if the box is the only thing on it: many UPSes report output watts, or a load percentage you can multiply by the rated watts.
- The GPU's own reading (`nvidia-smi --query-gpu=power.draw --format=csv -l 1`, or `powermetrics` on a Mac) leaves out the CPU, RAM, fans and power-supply losses. It's useful for comparing settings, not for cost.

## What moves the number

- **Model size and quantisation.** A 2026 preprint that measured 1B–7B models on a 16 GB consumer GPU found the smallest models used under 0.7 joules per token, and a 7B model up to 4.4× more energy per token than the most efficient one. Architecture and quantisation mattered as much as parameter count.
- **Power limits.** Generation is mostly memory-bound, so a moderate GPU power limit often costs little speed. One public test cut a card from 180 W to 150 W, kept essentially the same tokens per second, and gained about 16 % efficiency.
- **Reasoning-style models.** Tokens per kWh can look fine while energy per *answer* is high, because the model writes many hidden tokens before replying. If you care about cost per answer, measure that too.
- **Idle.** The biggest lever for a home box that isn't busy all day: the all-in rows above. Spinning down idle GPUs, or putting the model on a machine that idles low, can matter more than any tuning.
